Morphological analysis is the systematic study of the internal structure of words. English words are frequently built from smaller meaningful units called morphemes. A prefix such as re- may be attached to a base like write to express repetition, while a suffix such as -ness can turn the adjective happy into the noun happiness. Understanding these pieces allows both learners and machines to recover the underlying meaning of unfamiliar forms.

A rule-based analyzer decomposes an input word by proposing candidate splits and then validating them. The matcher searches for known prefixes and suffixes, preferring the longest possible matches so that misinterpretation is read as mis plus interpret plus ation rather than a collection of shorter fragments. Each candidate root is checked against a lexical database, which prevents the engine from accepting invented stems that merely look plausible.

Spelling often changes at the boundary between an affix and its base. The final y in happy becomes an i before the suffix ness, and the silent e in write disappears before ing. Such alternations are productive in English, and a practical analyzer must reverse them systematically. Reversing these changes is what makes accurate decomposition possible for forms the engine has never seen before.

Batch processing extends the same pipeline to entire documents. A corpus of hundreds or thousands of words can be submitted in a single request, and every token receives its own decomposition, rule trace and confidence score. Researchers use these outputs to compare approaches, to audit coverage of a vocabulary, and to export structured results for further statistical work.
